# Philosophy and practice of task expression

## Purpose

A well-expressed task lets a future reader recognize the intended action without reconstructing its meaning. Optimize clarity, fidelity, and low reading effort, rather than formality, a rigid sentence length, or a productivity score.

This reference owns expression. It can detect that an action has not been decided, but cannot decide the next action or invent project scope. A nicer sentence is not evidence of a clearer commitment if it silently changes the work.

## Principles

1. **Faithful before polished:** preserve actual intent and constraints, including supplied uncertainty.
2. **Observable:** express a step whose execution and finish can be recognized.
3. **Concrete:** identify the object, and recipient or context only when needed to distinguish the action.
4. **Enough context:** make a reminder understandable without copying its entire support material.
5. **One semantic unit:** avoid a title that conceals unrelated actions or a whole project. Flag the mismatch; do not manufacture a breakdown.
6. **Minimum useful wording:** remove vague padding and redundant labels without imposing an arbitrary length or language change.
7. **Honest incompleteness:** unresolved facts stay visible; fluent placeholders are not actual names, paths, dates, or agreements.

## Title expression

For a selected executable action, begin with an action verb plus a concrete object. Examples include “Draft the event invitation”, “Read the supplier's estimate”, “Compare the two renewal offers”, and “Send the approval request to the reviewer”. Reading, drafting, and comparing are observable when their object and practical finish are clear; “physical” does not exclude knowledge work.

Choose a verb that reflects the action already intended. If the input only says “Budget” or “Prepare launch”, several actions are possible. Do not select “Email the accountant” or “Draft the landing page” without evidence. Ask what action has been chosen, retain the outcome-level reminder, or show an explicitly unresolved drafting pattern for the user to complete.

Project outcome titles and Waiting For reminders are different representations. Preserve them as such; forcing every record into an imperative action title would falsely make outcomes or dependencies appear executable. Use the states module for how to express those supplied distinctions.

## Revision pipeline

Inspect supplied intent → identify representation type → preserve decision facts → improve verb/object/context wording → check meaning unchanged → mark remaining gaps → return the edit or hand off a missing method decision.

For “Email supplier” with supplied intent to request the revised estimate, “Request the revised estimate from the supplier by email” is faithful. If the purpose is not supplied, do not invent the revised estimate. “Email the supplier about [purpose not supplied]” is an illustrative unresolved draft, not a ready task or an instruction to send anything.

## Size and decomposition boundary

A title can expose that work is too broad to express as a selected action. Describe that gap and route next-action discovery to GTD or knowledge-work packet design to CODE. Do not construct subtask trees or decide an action-duration quota here. A known three-minute action remains three minutes; improving its wording does not enlarge it or reserve a calendar block.

## Theoretical limits

These are practical writing principles, not formal theorems or guaranteed productivity effects. The conventions are the user's local task-expression policy; they are not universal file/document rules or a substitute for commitment and planning decisions.

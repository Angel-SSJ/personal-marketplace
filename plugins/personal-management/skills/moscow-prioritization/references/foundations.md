# MoSCoW concepts and scope decisions

## Conditions for meaningful prioritization

First identify the intended outcome, horizon, real obligations, decision authority, available effort, and uncertainty. A horizon can be the current week or a deliverable stage; it bounds the scope decision without assigning a fake deadline or scheduling every task. If the horizon or intended outcome is unknown, keep categories provisional and clarify them rather than assign permanent Must labels.

## Category rules

| Category             | Test relative to the named outcome and horizon                                                                                                        | Planning consequence                                                                        |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Must Have            | Without this, the agreed minimum outcome is not viable, safe, or able to satisfy a genuine non-negotiable obligation; no acceptable workaround exists | Protect the necessary scope and its prerequisites; test feasibility before committing       |
| Should Have          | Important; omission hurts, but a viable workaround or acceptable temporary alternative exists                                                         | Negotiate the workaround and defer only with its consequences visible                       |
| Could Have           | Desirable, with lower omission impact                                                                                                                 | First candidate for removal if capacity tightens                                            |
| Won't Have this time | Explicitly excluded from this horizon by the appropriate decision maker                                                                               | Keep the exclusion visible; do not silently reintroduce it or call it permanently worthless |

Must is not “I really want this”, “it is old”, “it is short”, or “someone marked it urgent”. Should and Could do not promise guaranteed delivery. Won't means “not in this scope now”, not “Would Have”, completed, permanently canceled, or automatically an uncommitted possibility.

## Classification and challenge pipeline

1. Define the horizon and minimum useful outcome. Identify existing promises and real deadline constraints first.
2. Review the candidate work without assuming it all belongs in scope. Do not silently relabel accepted promises as excluded.
3. Ask what happens if each item is omitted and whether an acceptable workaround exists. Give a short omission-impact rationale.
4. Split an overly broad item into a necessary core and optional improvements when that changes the decision. Use only enough decomposition to distinguish the necessary core from optional work; avoid speculative detail.
5. Trace dependencies. A Must cannot depend on an excluded or discretionary prerequisite and still be a credible commitment. Include the prerequisite at the required scope level, find a viable alternative, or renegotiate the dependent outcome. Check prerequisite effort and avoid counting shared work twice.
6. Check effort, capacity, and uncertainty. If genuine Must work cannot fit, surface the conflict and renegotiate scope, capacity, horizon, or the actual agreement. A new label cannot create time or cancel a real obligation.
7. Agree exclusions and review them at the horizon boundary or when material facts change. Distinguish proposals from accepted scope decisions.

## Capacity and contingency

Use comparable effort units, not counts of items. Count necessary prerequisite effort and coordination; account for existing capacity reservations, known commitments, and realistic available capacity. Unknown estimates are unknown, not zero. Avoid false precision; use ranges where uncertainty matters.

The DSDM context recommends a Must effort share typically no greater than about 60%, with a meaningful Could pool often around 20% of planned active effort. This is contextual contingency guidance, not a compulsory personal quota, an exact 60/20/20 law, or proof that a plan fits. Won't items are excluded from active-scope effort. Small personal scopes can have a different sensible mix. Never invent optional work, add filler tasks, or demote a genuine obligation merely to improve a percentage.

Two independent checks are needed: does total work fit actual capacity with uncertainty, and is sufficient scope negotiable if estimates worsen? A favorable category split cannot make eleven hours fit eight. A plan fitting the estimated hours exactly may still be fragile.

When pressure increases, examine Could first, then Should with its workable alternative, while protecting the real minimum. If the minimum itself is infeasible, communicate the conflict rather than pretend guaranteed delivery. Quality or necessary verification is not an expendable shortcut hidden under optional scope.

## Multiple horizons, focus, and state

A deliverable can be Must for a project but Won't in the current week if later completion is genuinely viable. State the horizon whenever this distinction matters. Do not use it to ignore a deadline within the current horizon.

Keep the scope judgment independent of any separate execution priorities or scheduling policy. A required item may be blocked and not currently executable. No category determines a unique execution order or supplies an action-selection procedure; leave those decisions to the user's chosen execution workflow.

## Worked capacity example

Eight ten-minute tasks plus two five-hour tasks total eleven hours twenty minutes, before any uncounted coordination. With eight hours available, “all Must” is a conflict to investigate, not a workable plan. Challenge omission impact and workaround options, include required prerequisites, then negotiate a genuinely feasible minimum. Do not drop a true promise to meet a ratio. The categories do not assign clock slots.

## Evidence and limits

This operational synthesis covers MoSCoW scope management associated with DSDM and the Agile Business Consortium. The categories and effort/dependency checks are explained here in full. The method offers a negotiation framework, not an optimal-scheduling theorem or a personal calendar policy. Respect any separate user execution/calendar policy; this method does not prescribe personal scheduling blocks, task wording, file structure, or storage paths.
